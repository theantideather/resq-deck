    The first minutes

    Most emergencies happen where the help isn't

    Not in a resuscitation bay with a team. In a clinic room with one practitioner, one assistant, and a patient who was fine four minutes ago.

    7 lakh

    Sudden cardiac deaths in India each year.

    2%

    Of Indian adults know how to perform CPR.

    7–10%

    Lost chance of survival for every minute that passes without CPR.

    Chance of survival, relative to CPR starting at once

    Band drawn from the 7–10% per minute figure. The practitioner has to recognise the condition, recall the protocol and dose, keep hands on the patient, call an ambulance and brief the hospital. ResQ takes everything except the hands-on part.

    When you say "Activate ResQ"

    Two things start at the same time

    One is fast, so you are never waiting. One is thorough, so you are not relying on speed alone. They meet only at steps where a change is safe to make.

    00:00

    It listens, and it already knows who is in the chair

    The wake word is recognised on the unit itself, so nothing leaves the room before you ask. ResQ pulls up the patient from the appointment you are in.

    00:02

    The first instruction, before you have finished describing it

    One clear step at a time, read out loud, with the reason, a metronome for compressions and a running clock.

    00:04

    Help is already moving

    ResQ requests an ambulance, alerts a hospital that can treat this case, and tells you how far away they are. You never stop to make a call.

    00:06

    A council of specialists reads the case

    Specialist agents work through what you described, the patient's record and any photograph your assistant sends. They argue it out rather than agreeing too quickly.

    00:42

    A correction arrives when it is safe to hear it

    ResQ never cuts across compressions. It waits for a step where a change is safe, lands before the step it concerns, and says what changed and why.

    Why a spectrum beats a single answer

    Every agent answers in the same structure, so the answers can be compared, weighted and argued with, and so a rare, lethal possibility survives a vote it would otherwise lose.

    Everything is timed
    Every instruction, drug and confirmation carries a timestamp. At the end there is a handover summary for the hospital and an honest record of what was done.
    
    
    Written for who is in the room
    Protocols are written at different skill levels. Someone without a prescriber's training is never told to give a prescription drug.
    
    
    Alongside the emergency services
    ResQ calls 108 when nothing faster accepts. It supports the person giving care; it does not stand in for emergency services.
    
  
    

    


    
  
    
    
    
      
    The medical access card
    
      
    The catch that saved him only works if he is your patient
    
      
    ResQ stopped that nitrate because the clinic's own records listed the sildenafil. A stranger who collapses in your waiting room has no record you can read. The access card is how that stops being true.

    The key to a patient's record is split three ways, so no single party holds it. Nobody can open the record alone, including us.
    
        
    
          
    
            1
            
    
              
    The public anchor
    
              
    An on-chain identity anchor that says a record exists and where its shares live. It holds no medical information at all, and it is useless on its own.
    
            
    
          
    
          
    
            2
            
    
              
    The clinic's share
    
              
    Held inside the treating practice's patient management system. It travels with the clinic, not with the patient, and it never leaves that system.
    
            
    
          
    
          
    
            3
            
    
              
    The patient's share
    
              
    Held by the patient, on the card in their wallet and mirrored in their own app. This is the share that makes the record theirs rather than the hospital's.
    
            
    
          
    
        
    
      
    
    
    

    
    
      
    
        two shares of three
        
    The emergency view opens in seconds
    
        
    Blood group, allergies, current medications, active conditions, emergency contact and any advance directive. Enough to stop ResQ from telling you to give the wrong drug. Not the patient's full history, and not available for anything except an open emergency session.
    
      
    
      
    
        all three shares
        
    The complete record opens with the patient
    
        
    Combining all three shares derives a twelve word phrase that decrypts the whole record: notes, imaging, prescriptions, referrals, every clinic the patient has ever attended. It takes the patient's own share, so it happens with them, not around them.
    
      
    
    
    

    
    
      
    Every opening is written down
    Break glass access is logged on the chain as an entry the patient can read: who opened it, at what time, under which emergency session, and what they saw. The log cannot be edited afterwards, including by us.
    
      
    The record itself is never on the chain
    Only key shares, pointers and the access log are on chain. The medical record is encrypted and held off chain, so a patient can still have data corrected or erased as the law requires.
    
      
    It works for a stranger
    A patient who has never visited your practice arrives with a card. The emergency view opens, ResQ reads their medications, and the contraindication check runs exactly as it would for a patient of twenty years.
    
    
    

    
    The access card is in design. Nothing described here is live yet, and the break glass rules need review under the DPDP Act 2023 before any patient data touches the system.
    
  
    

    


    
  
    
    
    The clinical library
    
    
    What it knows, and who wrote it
    
    
    Protocols are authored and reviewed by practising clinicians against current guidance, then versioned like code. A general model cannot safely produce a dose under time pressure. A reviewed protocol can.
    
  
    
  
    
    
    250Dental emergencies and procedural complications
    
    
    20Core medical emergencies, from anaphylaxis to cardiac arrest
    
    
    10Equipment failures that stop a procedure mid‑way
    
    
    10+Indian languages, including colloquial descriptions of symptoms
    
  
    
  
    
    
    Written for who is in the room
    The same emergency reads differently for a specialist, a nurse and a bystander. Someone without a prescriber's training is never told to give a prescription drug.
    
    
    Versioned and dated
    Every protocol carries an author, a reviewer and a review date. When guidance changes, the change is traceable and every unit gets it.
    
    
    It improves with use
    Anonymised outcomes sharpen the protocols. The library is the thing the subscription is actually for.
    
  
    

    


    
  
    
    
    
      
    The network
    
      
    Guidance is only half of it
    
      
    Responders are chosen by how fast they can arrive and whether they can treat this particular case, not by distance alone. A nearer hospital that cannot open a blocked artery is the wrong hospital.
    
    
    
    
    
      
    
        ground transport
        
    Ambulances
    
        
    Requested in parallel with an acceptance handshake, tracked live, and passed to the next crew if nobody accepts. Falls back to 108 when nothing faster answers.
    
      
    
      
    
        air transport
        
    Air ambulance
    
        
    For transfers beyond ground range or ground time. Discussions are under way with air ambulance and ride hailing operators for transport capacity. Nothing is signed yet.
    
      
    
      
    
        rural reach
        
    Primary health centres
    
        
    India's network of roughly 30,000 primary health centres, used as stabilisation points before transfer and as forward stock for emergency drugs.
    
      
    
      
    
        the receiving end
        
    Hospitals
    
        
    The receiving team gets what happened, what was given and when, and what to prepare. The note is revised again if the council's view changes on the way in.
    
      
    
      
    
        instruments
        
    Connected devices
    
        
    Pulse oximeters, glucometers, blood pressure cuffs and wearables feed readings straight into the case, so numbers are not read aloud and retyped under pressure.
    
      
    
      
    
        supply
        
    Drugs and equipment
    
        
    Tomorrow's appointments are checked against the clinic's own stock and expiry dates, with resupply through pharmacies and kit delivery.
    
      
    
    
    
  
    

    


    
  
    
    
    Market
    
    
    A narrow beachhead inside a large market
    
    
    Dentistry first, deliberately. Procedures are elective, the patient is supine, complications are well documented, and one practitioner carries the whole emergency alone.

    3 lakh+Registered dentists in India, before counting physicians, clinics and corporate health rooms
    
    
    
    
    
      
    Emergency response is the part of that market nobody has built properly, because doing it needs three things at once: clinical content a doctor will trust, hardware that works hands free, and a dispatch network that actually answers.
    
      
    A dental clinic is also the cleanest place to prove the product works. If one practitioner plus ResQ handles an emergency better than one practitioner alone, the same argument carries to every other setting where somebody is on their own.
